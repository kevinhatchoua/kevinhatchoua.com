What happens when we build something and it works as intended? What happens when it doesn't?

When we design, we change something. A moment. A decision. A habit. A culture.

That is a powerful thing, and with great power comes great responsibility. As we create experiences, what exactly should we pay attention to?

- Did we make things easier or more confusing?
- Did we reduce stress, or introduce friction?
- Did we give someone the confidence to keep going… or the frustration to give up?

Designers hold the power to shape behavior and perception. That is why we must design with intent and measure the impact.

## The "Why" behind the work

![Before and after of Abeckett Street temporary urban square in Melbourne](/images/blog/metrics-abeckett-square.jpg)

*Source: [Peter Elliot Pty Ltd Architecture](https://peterelliott.com.au/projects/urban-design/abeckett-temporary-urban-square)*

When we design an experience, we are placing a bet. A bet that what we're building will improve life in some way, simplify a process, reduce anxiety, spark joy, and remove friction.

From a UX perspective, a good bet is measured by how meaningfully we improved the human experience. When we get it right for the people we serve, the money tends to follow. Happy users return. They recommend. They stick around. Good design creates trust, and trust drives business.

So before we create anything, we must first ask:

- Why are we doing this?
- Who is this for?
- What are they trying to achieve?
- Whose life did we make better, and how?
- Lastly, what would success look like in their words, not ours?

Once we've grounded ourselves in the "why," we can begin to talk about how we measure outcomes and the user experience.

## Measuring the experience

When we talk about measuring the experience, there are 3 key types of metrics worth paying attention to:

1. **Success metrics**
   1. Did the person get what they came for?
   2. Did they reach that "Ah-ha!"?
   3. Did they leave feeling empowered?
2. **Progress metrics**
   1. Are we getting better at helping them over time?
   2. Are they navigating more easily, asking fewer questions, and completing steps with less friction?
3. **Problem value metrics**
   1. What happens when the experience goes wrong?
   2. Do people give up? Do they call support? Do they feel confused, anxious, or excluded?
   3. And what's the cost to them, and to us?

They enable us to discuss impact, not just interaction. And through it all, we must ask what their experience was before? What is their experience now?

## The UX metrics trap

We love our KPIs and metrics! They are a great representation of what's happening. But here's the trap: They are only as valuable as the outcomes they're tied to. Without context, they're just vanity mirrors.

Let's break it down:

- **Measurement:** Tracks actual UX changes. Did users complete tasks faster, with fewer errors, or less confusion? It's about real shifts in behavior, not assumptions.
- **KPIs:** Are signals we choose to monitor; they are indicators that help us understand how close we are to the desired user and business outcomes.
- **Analytics:** Captures in-product behavior. They tell us what people did, but not why they did it, or how they felt afterward.

It's not enough to know what happened; we also need to understand why it mattered. Metrics without meaning are noise. Tie them to an outcome, or let them go.

## Good UX is observable.

You don't need to squint at charts to see it. You feel it.

In reduced confusion, in the shift from friction to flow, and in the joy and delights that come when things just… work.

## So Why Does This Matter?

If we're not measuring the right things, we risk building the wrong things. Things that may look good on the surface, but don't actually help people.

Design has always been about intention. About making choices that lead to better outcomes, not just for the business, but for the person on the other side.

So when we talk about metrics that matter, it is about being accountable to the people we're designing for. Metrics should help us see clearly, not just where people go, but how they feel when they get there.

- **Useful** - Does it solve a real need?
- **Usable** - Is it accessible, intuitive, and empowering?
- **Used** - Are people choosing it, returning to it, relying on it

Let's build with purpose.

Let's measure what moves people.

Let's make things better by making better things.
